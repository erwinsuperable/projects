Rebuilding, not retrofitting

For most of my career, product design has followed a familiar shape. A requirement arrives, someone interprets it, I design in Figma, the design is approved and handed over, and eventually it’s built. Each step makes sense on its own. Together, they create a process where knowledge is constantly being translated, and a little of it is lost every time it moves.

Through my work at Chalhoub Group, on our Factory Design System, multibrand and white label experiences, and the MUSE loyalty experience, I’ve started to look at that shape differently. AI tools have made it possible to produce interfaces much faster. But speed was never really the problem I was trying to solve.

I’m not simply introducing AI into our existing design process. I’m rebuilding the process to become agentic, where Figma, our Factory Design System, business requirements, documentation, and source code are connected through specialized agents. Designers remain responsible for the decisions that shape the customer experience.

This article is a record of that work in progress. Some of what follows comes from experiments I’ve run with engineering colleagues. Much of it is still a proposal: a direction I’m working toward rather than a system that exists today. I’ve tried to be clear about which is which, and the case studies carry a marker for their current status.

The future of product design isn’t just about producing interfaces faster. It’s about building better systems for understanding problems, defining experiences, maintaining design knowledge, and translating design decisions into functioning products.

The problem with traditional design

The traditional process doesn’t struggle because people do their jobs badly. It struggles because it was designed around handovers. Information moves from a business requirement to a product manager, to a designer, into Figma, through approval and handover, into engineering, and finally into production.

At every one of those boundaries, something has to be re-explained. Context that was obvious in a requirements discussion doesn’t always make it into the ticket. A decision made in a design review lives in someone’s memory rather than in the file. By the time an implementation question reaches the designer, the conversation that would have answered it may have happened weeks earlier.

Mapped this way, the problems are rarely about the quality of an individual screen. They’re about missing context, incomplete documentation, inconsistencies between designs, feedback that arrives too late to be cheap, and implementations that drift from what was designed. None of those problems is solved by designing faster. If anything, producing more screens more quickly gives these gaps more places to appear.

The documentation problem

Documentation is where these gaps become most visible. Too often, documentation is treated as a phase: something that happens after the design is done, usually under time pressure, and rarely revisited after launch.

The result is predictable. Specifications describe the happy path and leave interaction behavior implied. Guidelines say how components should be used, but not why. Changes approved during implementation never make it back into the design file or the documentation, so the next person starts from an outdated picture.

One of the challenges I’ve encountered is that design knowledge lives in too many places at once: Confluence pages, Jira tickets, Figma comments, meeting notes, and the memory of whoever was in the room. Each holds part of the truth. None holds all of it.

What I’m trying to establish is a process where documentation isn’t written once at the end but maintained continuously as part of the work, with agents helping to draft, structure and update it, and designers approving what it says.

Introducing the agentic process

An agentic process, as I use the term, is one where specialized AI agents take on well-defined parts of the work, such as retrieving requirements, analyzing scenarios, generating editable designs, drafting documentation, and comparing design with code, while the people involved define the goals, set the rules, and make the decisions.

The difference from simply using AI is structure. Instead of asking a chat tool for help whenever I’m stuck, I’m trying to connect the tools to each other and to the sources of truth we already have: requirements in Confluence and Jira, components and variables in Figma, foundations in Factory, and the implementation in source code.

The stages still exist. Requirements still need to be understood, designs still need to be created, and engineering still builds the product. What changes is that each stage produces structured output the next stage can use, that there are feedback connections back to earlier stages, and that the Senior Product Designer is present throughout, reviewing outputs and approving direction.

It’s important to be honest about where this stands. This isn’t a fully automated pipeline, and I don’t think it should become one. The diagram describes the process I’m working toward, not one that runs end to end today.

The five agentic pillars

To make the idea practical, I’ve organized it into five pillars. Each has a clear purpose, a set of tools, an expected output, and, most importantly, a human responsibility that doesn’t move to an agent.

The pillars aren’t strictly sequential. Discovery informs design, design generates documentation, engineering collaboration surfaces discrepancies that send work back to design, and governance runs across all of them. Naming them separately helps me see where agents can genuinely help, and where a human decision has to stay in the loop.

Factory Design System

Factory is our shared design system: the foundation for a multibrand digital ecosystem. It’s where an agentic process either works or falls apart, because an agent can only generate consistent designs if the system it draws from is consistent and well described.

The challenge with any multibrand system is maintaining consistency and scalability across components, variables and brands at the same time. Every brand needs to feel like itself. But if each one drifts into its own set of components, the system stops being shared in any meaningful sense.

My approach is to lean on structure: foundation tokens that feed semantic variables, brand configurations that resolve those variables differently, reusable components that only reference semantic values, and experience patterns built from those components. Alongside that structure, I’m exploring Markdown guidelines that describe how the system should be used, so an agent generating a design has the same guidance a designer would.

White label experience

Closely related to Factory is our work on white label experiences: reusable digital journeys that can be presented under different brands.

The problem is a familiar one. When similar journeys, such as sign-in, are designed independently for each brand, they slowly diverge. Not because anyone decided they should be different, but because nobody decided they should be the same. Those differences add design effort, engineering effort, and inconsistency for customers.

The approach is to separate reusable interaction patterns from brand-specific visual presentation. The structure of a journey, meaning the order of fields, the validation rules, the error handling and the focus order, is designed once. The presentation, meaning color, typography, shape and imagery, is configured per brand.

From requirements to Figma

Product requirements are distributed across documentation and delivery tools: business context in Confluence, scope and acceptance criteria in Jira. Translating that information into a design is work designers have always done by hand: reading, cross-referencing, summarizing, and only then opening Figma.

That manual translation is repetitive, and every time information is copied by hand there’s a chance it changes. So I’ve been exploring how agents could support each step: Atlassian Rovo to retrieve and connect requirement context, Claude and ChatGPT to analyze it and challenge assumptions, Figma to hold the generated, editable designs, Factory as the reference for validation, and a designer review at the end.

When requirements are ambiguous, an agent can produce a confident-looking design built on that ambiguity, which is exactly why the last step is a designer, not a validator.

MUSE loyalty experience

Loyalty experiences show why discovery matters. On the surface, redeeming a reward looks simple. Underneath, it depends on business rules, eligibility, availability, and transactions that can fail in ways a happy-path design never shows.

The problem is that complex rules and user scenarios are easy to overlook when design begins too early. Start drawing screens before understanding the scenarios, and the common case gets designed beautifully while the edge cases are discovered during implementation.

My approach on MUSE has been to investigate requirements first, identify the scenarios the experience needs to support, and establish clearer interaction criteria before committing to a design. That is exactly the kind of work agentic discovery is meant to support.

Text specified at one size can look different on a device, and understanding why means looking at several factors rather than assuming a single cause. I come back to this in the section on design-to-code validation.

Reverse engineering an existing portal

Not every project starts with a requirement. Sometimes the product already exists, but an accurate, editable design representation doesn’t. That was the situation with an existing internal portal: there was a working codebase, but no design file that reliably reflected what had been built.

This is one of the clearest experiments I can point to. An engineer extracted the existing codebase and used Claude to help translate the interface into Figma layers. The result was a set of editable layers representing the existing interface: a starting point rather than a finished design file.

I’d rather describe that honestly as the next step than present it as done.

Design-to-code validation

Design and code have a habit of becoming two competing versions of the truth. The Figma file says one thing, the implementation does another, and nobody is quite sure which is right.

What I’m trying to establish instead is a loop: Figma design, design specifications, source code, implementation validation and approved changes, each connected to the next in both directions. Design intent is compared with the implementation. Discrepancies are detected and described. A person reviews them and decides how to resolve them. Approved changes flow back into both the design and the documentation.

The typography example is deliberately framed as a validation scenario, not a diagnosis. A gap between a 16px specification and an implementation that looks smaller could come from the font family, font metrics, weight, native rendering, line height or scaling configuration. An agent can help gather evidence across those areas. Deciding the right outcome, whether that’s changing the implementation, changing the specification, or accepting and documenting a platform difference, still needs a designer and an engineer looking at it together.

Markdown skills

One of the most practical things I’ve been experimenting with is writing Markdown-based skills: reusable instructions that describe how an agent should carry out a particular kind of design work.

The idea is simple. Much of what experienced designers know is procedural. Check the requirements. Inspect existing components. Use semantic variables rather than hardcoded values. Don’t duplicate what already exists. Ask when something is unresolved. That knowledge usually lives in our heads or in scattered guidelines. Writing it as a skill turns it into something an agent can follow consistently, and something the team can review and improve.

It helps to separate three things that are easy to blur together.

A skill doesn’t replace the design system, and it doesn’t replace judgment. It encodes a process. And like any process, it needs maintaining: when Factory changes, the skills that reference it have to change too.

Design acceptance criteria

Acceptance criteria are usually written from a functional perspective: the feature does what the ticket says. What’s often missing are UX acceptance criteria, the conditions under which an experience is actually acceptable for customers.

In the process I’m working toward, defining UX acceptance criteria is an explicit output of discovery and documentation, not an afterthought during testing. The criteria describe states, feedback, accessibility expectations and edge cases in a form that engineering can test against and agents can check designs against.

Writing criteria this way forces unresolved questions to the surface early. If I can’t write the criterion for what happens when a transaction fails, that’s a sign the scenario hasn’t been designed yet.

The connected tools ecosystem

The tools in this process aren’t competitors, and I’m not trying to pick a winner. Each has a role it’s particularly suited to, and the value comes from how they connect.

ChatGPT helps with research, reasoning, ideation and scenario analysis. Claude is where I’ve focused structured workflows, code interpretation, Markdown skills and design translation. Rovo brings Atlassian knowledge and workflow assistance closer to where requirements live. Jira tracks delivery and acceptance criteria. Confluence holds business requirements and documentation. Figma holds editable designs, components, variables and prototypes. Factory holds our shared foundations and guidelines. And source code is the product as it actually exists.

The Product Designer’s evolving role

The obvious question about this work is whether agents will replace designers. I don’t think that’s the right question. The more useful one is how the role changes when much of the execution can be supported by agents.

The traditional responsibilities don’t disappear. I still interpret requirements, research solutions and review implementation. What changes is where the emphasis sits: more time goes toward defining problems and outcomes, establishing strategy, writing reusable instructions, governing how the design system is used, validating what agents produce, and keeping design and code aligned.

The Senior Product Designer is not being replaced by agents. The role is evolving toward greater orchestration, systems thinking and decision accountability.

Lessons learned

These are the reflections I keep coming back to. None of them is final, but each has changed how I approach the work.

Speed without structure creates more problems.

Generating screens quickly is easy. Generating the right screens, consistently, within a system, is not. Without structure, speed just moves problems downstream faster.

A polished design is not necessarily a validated solution.

AI-generated designs can look finished long before the underlying scenarios are understood. Visual polish can hide unanswered questions.

AI can amplify inconsistencies when guidelines are incomplete.

An agent follows the system it’s given. If the design system or its guidelines are inconsistent, the agent reproduces those inconsistencies at scale.

Documentation must become part of the ongoing process.

Documentation written once at the end starts going out of date immediately. It has to be maintained alongside the work.

Design and code should not become competing versions of the truth.

The goal isn’t for one to win. It’s for both to stay connected through a process that makes discrepancies visible and resolves them deliberately.

Human judgment remains essential.

Agents can analyze, generate and compare. Deciding what’s right for the customer, and owning that decision, remains a human responsibility.

Reusable skills must be maintained.

A skill that describes an outdated system can do more harm than no skill at all. Skills need owners, reviews and versions, like any other part of the system.

Design systems need governance.

The more work agents do with a design system, the more it matters who can change it, how changes are approved, and how unsupported patterns are identified.

Future vision

What I’m working toward is a shift from disconnected design activities to a continuously connected Agentic Product Design Process: business, product, design, AI agents, design systems, engineering and production working from shared context, with feedback flowing in every direction rather than only forward.

That doesn’t mean full autonomy. In every version of this I can imagine, people define the problems, make the decisions, and remain accountable for the experiences customers receive. Agents make the connections between those people and systems stronger, faster and easier to maintain.

The biggest opportunity AI creates for Product Design isn’t simply the ability to produce more. It’s the opportunity to rethink how we work, how we collaborate, and how we maintain the relationship between the experiences we imagine and the products we actually build.

Conclusion

When I started exploring AI in my work, I expected it to change how quickly I could design. More and more, it’s changing what I need to design. The interfaces still matter, but so do the instructions agents follow, the system they draw from, the criteria that define success, and the process that connects all of it to engineering.

That’s why I think of this work as going beyond Figma. Figma remains central to how I design. But much of the most important design work now happens around it: in how requirements are understood, how knowledge is maintained, and how decisions travel from an idea to a product.

We’re no longer just designing interfaces. We’re designing the systems, instructions, and processes that enable those interfaces to be built correctly.
